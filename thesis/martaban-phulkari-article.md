## Introduction

In many Punjabi homes, there was once a heavy glazed jar in the corner of the kitchen or on the rooftop. It held mango achar curing in the sun, lemon pickle soaking in mustard oil, rice kept safe from insects, and sugar kept dry through the monsoon. This jar is the **martaban**.

For generations, the martaban was part of daily life. Today most young people have never used one. Plastic containers have taken its place. They are cheap and light, but they are not made to hold oily, acidic foods for months.

My thesis project began with one question: **How can a traditional food-storage jar become meaningful again for a new generation?** My answer was to bring together two Punjabi heritage crafts, the martaban and **phulkari** embroidery, in one ceramic piece that is safe for food and beautiful enough to keep on display.

## The Martaban: A Jar With a Story

The word *martaban* is linked to the port town of Martaban (now Mottama) in Myanmar. Large glazed storage jars were traded from there across Asia for centuries. Over time, the name came to mean any large glazed jar used to store food in South Asian homes.

The martaban worked because of how it was made:

- **A glazed inner surface** that does not absorb oil, vinegar or salt
- **Thick clay walls** that keep the temperature inside steady
- **An opaque body** that keeps out sunlight, so food does not spoil as fast
- **A narrow mouth and a lid** that limit air and moisture

These qualities made it ideal for pickles (achar), murabba, ghee, rice, sugar and grains. Households could prepare food in season and store it for the whole year.

Most martabans, however, were made in one standard style: a plain jar in brown or green. There was little design variation and little that spoke to younger buyers. As kitchens changed, the jar was forgotten.

## Phulkari: The Flower Work of Punjab

**Phulkari** comes from two words: *phul*, meaning flower, and *kari*, meaning work. Together they mean "flower work." It is a hand-embroidery tradition of Punjab. Women stitched bright silk floss threads onto coarse cotton cloth, mostly from the back of the fabric, using long, straight darning stitches. Rows of parallel stitches built up geometric flowers, and the sheen of the silk made the patterns shine.

### History

Historians are not sure where phulkari began. Some think it came from Iran, where a similar floral embroidery called *gulkari* existed. Others connect it to communities from Central Asia who settled in the Punjab. A seventh-century chronicle by the court writer Bana describes people "embroidering flowers and leaves on the cloth from the reverse side," which matches the phulkari technique. The earliest known use of the word *phulkari* itself is in **Waris Shah's *Heer Ranjha*** (18th century). It describes Heer's wedding trousseau, which includes phulkari garments. In 1880, Flora Annie Steel wrote the first detailed English account, recording the different styles across the Punjab.

Phulkari was deeply personal. When a girl was born, her mother and grandmother often began embroidering phulkaris for her dowry. A fully covered piece, where the embroidery hides the base cloth, is called a **bagh** (garden). Phulkaris were worn at weddings, births and festivals, and passed down as family treasures.

### From Haryana to Multan

My research traced the style of phulkari I studied to the **Haryana** region, in present-day India. Before 1947, Haryana, Multan and the rest of the region all belonged to one undivided Punjab, so craftswomen, families, traders and textiles moved freely between them. Phulkari spread through this whole region, including western areas such as Hazara, Rawalpindi and **Multan**.

I could not find any record giving an exact year when phulkari reached Multan. It seems to have arrived slowly through marriage, migration and trade within the Punjab, not on one date. The **Partition of 1947** also carried skills and pieces across the new border as families migrated. Today, phulkari is a shared heritage of Punjab on both sides of the border, and Multan, already famous for its crafts, is one of the places in Pakistan where it lives on.

The lack of written records was itself one of the biggest challenges of this project.

## The Concept: Phulkari Meets the Martaban

My idea was to **translate phulkari from thread to clay**. I wanted to keep the martaban's useful form and give it the colour, pattern and identity of phulkari. The result is a jar that:

1. **Preserves food** the way the traditional martaban did
2. **Represents Punjabi culture** through phulkari motifs
3. **Attracts young people** through a fresh, decorative look
4. **Gives a healthier alternative** to plastic storage

## Materials and Process

### Stoneware clay

I chose **stoneware clay** for the body. Stoneware is fired at high temperatures, which makes it dense, strong and far less porous than ordinary earthenware. It does not absorb oil or moisture easily, so pickles, rice and sugar stay fresh and hygienic.

![Figure 5: Forming the jar in stoneware clay.](images/fig05-forming-stoneware.jpg)
*Figure 5: Forming the jar in stoneware clay.*

### Food-safe glaze

The glaze is the surface that touches the food, so food safety guided every decision. Many traditional glazes contained lead, which can leach into acidic foods like pickles. I used a **food-safe glaze recipe with no harmful chemicals**. I mixed the colouring into this food-safe glaze base so that even the decorative colours are safe.

![Figure 6: Food-safe glaze and colour test tiles.](images/fig06-glaze-tests.jpg)
*Figure 6: Food-safe glaze and colour test tiles.*

### Materials used

| Material | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Stoneware clay | Strong, dense, non-porous jar body |
| Food-safe glaze | Safe inner and outer surface for food contact |
| Colours mixed into the food-safe glaze | Phulkari colours (reds, oranges, yellows and greens) without unsafe chemicals |

### Translating embroidery into ceramics

To give the jar the feeling of embroidery, I had to recreate in clay and glaze what a needle does in cloth: build patterns from **many fine parallel lines** that catch the light the way silk floss does.

## Health and Everyday Benefits

- **No plastic contact.** Food does not touch plastic, which can release chemicals, especially with oil, acid or heat.
- **Food-safe surface.** The lead-free glaze is suitable for acidic pickles.
- **Supports home-made food.** Families can store pickles, murabba, rice and sugar they prepare themselves, with control over salt, oil and preservatives. This matters for health-conscious people watching their **blood pressure and cholesterol**.
- **Long-lasting and eco-friendly.** One ceramic jar can last for decades, while plastic containers crack, stain and end up in landfills.

## Challenges I Faced

**1. Translating thread into ceramics.**
Phulkari is soft, flexible and made of shiny silk threads. Clay is hard and glaze flows when fired. Turning embroidery into a ceramic surface was very difficult.

**2. Creating parallel lines in clay.**
Phulkari's beauty comes from its tight, even parallel stitches. Making fine, straight, parallel lines on a curved clay surface, and keeping them sharp through glazing and firing, was one of the hardest technical problems.

**3. Capturing the "thread feel."**
Even when the pattern was right, it often looked painted instead of stitched. Getting the texture and sheen of thread took repeated experiments.

**4. Achieving the essence of phulkari.**
Copying a motif is not enough. Phulkari has a spirit of celebration, warmth and womanhood. Bringing that feeling to the jar meant repeating the work many times.

**5. Lack of research resources.**
Very little written material on phulkari is available, especially in Pakistan. Much of the documented history comes from Indian Punjab and Haryana, and records of how it spread to Multan are almost nonexistent. I had to piece the history together from books, museum collections, online archives and conversations.

**6. Time and repetition.**
The final piece came only after making the work **many times over more than eight months**. Each trial taught me something about the clay, the glaze, the lines and the colours.

## Purpose: Craft Revival and the Aesthetic Industry

This project is more than a single jar. Its goal is to **create awareness of craft revival** and show that traditional crafts can enter the modern **aesthetic and design industry**. When young people see a martaban decorated with phulkari, I hope they recognise their own culture and feel proud to use it.

Bringing two endangered traditions together helps keep both alive. The martaban survives through new design, and phulkari finds a new material to live in.

---

## Conclusion

The phulkari martaban joins my grandmother's kitchen and a modern home, embroidery and clay, and Punjab's past and its future. Made from stoneware and finished with a food-safe glaze, it keeps food fresh and safe while carrying the colours and memory of Punjabi women's needlework.
