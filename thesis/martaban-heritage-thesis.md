# Thesis Framework: Preserving Heritage Through the Martaban

**Working title:** *Martaban Reimagined: Preserving a Ceramic Food-Storage Heritage for Contemporary Kitchens*

**Thesis statement:** The martaban, a glazed ceramic jar used across Pakistan and North India to store achar (pickles), murabba, ghee and water, holds both craft knowledge and food culture. As plastic and glass replace it, that knowledge is being lost. This thesis records the traditional jar, analyses why it works, and redesigns it so it stays useful in today's homes while keeping its craft roots.

---

## 1. Background & Research

| Area | What to find out |
|---|---|
| History | Where the name comes from (the port of Martaban/Mottama, Myanmar, which traded large glazed jars), how the jar spread through South Asian trade, and how it became part of household life |
| Craft centres | Pottery clusters that still make storage jars, for example in Punjab and Sindh. Visit them and record names, families and methods |
| Food culture | Pickling methods: mustard-oil and vinegar achar, sun-curing, seasonal batches (mango, lemon, carrot, chilli) |
| Why it works | Glazed non-porous interior, opaque body that keeps light out, thick walls that hold a steady temperature, narrow mouth that limits air exposure, cloth-tied or lidded closure |
| Why it is declining | Weight, fragility, cost, smaller urban kitchens, cheap plastic and glass, fewer young potters |
| Risks | Traditional lead-based glazes can leach into acidic food such as pickles. This is a key reason to redesign with lead-free, food-safe glazes |

**Methods:** artisan interviews, household surveys (who still uses martabans and why), photo and video documentation, and collecting or measuring existing jars.

---

## 2. Drawing Studio: Observe and Document

- Observational drawings of traditional martabans in different sizes, glazes and lid types
- Ethnographic sketches of potters at work: wheel, kiln, glazing, drying racks
- Sketches of how the jar is used: filling, oiling, sun-curing on rooftops, serving
- Form analysis: proportion studies (height to belly width to neck), profile curves, silhouette catalogue
- Pattern and motif studies from regional surface decoration (floral, geometric, Multani blue-and-white references)
- Mood board and visual journal

**Output:** sketchbook, measured drawings of 5–10 existing jars, motif library.

---

## 3. Shaping Studio: Form Exploration

- Quick form studies in clay, foam or plaster (aim for 20+ variations)
- Explore the mouth and neck (pouring, scooping with a ladle), the lid (fit, seal, grip), handles or lugs (lifting heavy filled jars), and the base (stability)
- Family of sizes: small table jar, medium kitchen jar, large seasonal batch jar
- Ergonomic tests: hand reach inside, weight when full, carrying
- Choose 2–3 directions and refine them to scale models

**Output:** form-study models, a refined prototype set, a photo record of the evolution.

---

## 4. Production Drawing Studio: Technical Drawings

- Orthographic views (front, top, side) with dimensions
- Section drawings showing wall thickness, base thickness and lid seating
- Lid and seal detail, for example a groove for a silicone or cork gasket
- Shrinkage allowance: draw at "wet" size so the jar reaches its final size after drying and firing (often roughly 10–13% depending on the clay body; test with your own clay)
- Mould drawings: plaster mould parts, split lines, pour spout (for slip casting)
- Exploded views of the jar, lid, gasket and ladle
- Title block, scale, materials and finish notes

**Output:** a full set of drawings ready for a workshop or factory.

---

## 5. Technique: Materials and Process

| Topic | Notes |
|---|---|
| Clay body | Earthenware (traditional, porous, lower firing) compared with stoneware (denser, stronger, less porous) |
| Forming | Wheel throwing (craft or one-off), coiling (large jars), slip casting or jiggering (repeat production) |
| Glaze | **Lead-free, food-safe, acid-resistant** glaze inside. The outer glaze can carry traditional colour and motifs |
| Firing | Typical ranges: earthenware about 1000–1100 °C, stoneware about 1200–1280 °C. Bisque fire first, then glaze fire |
| Decoration | Hand-painted motifs, sgraffito, stamps, or slip trailing that refers back to regional patterns |
| Accessories | Wooden or ceramic ladle, cloth cover with tie (traditional), gasketed lid (modern) |

**Output:** test tiles, glaze trials, firing logs, and a comparison chart of what worked.

---

## 6. Production & Technology: Making It Real

- Choose a production method (slip-cast batch with a hand-finished glaze is a good balance of scale and craft)
- Make plaster master moulds and working moulds
- Quality tests:
  - **Lead/cadmium leach test** on the glaze (essential for pickles)
  - Water absorption and porosity
  - Thermal shock (sun-curing, then a cool indoor kitchen)
  - Lid seal test (oil leakage, air tightness)
  - Drop and chip test
- Costing: clay, glaze, firing energy, labour, packaging and price
- Collaboration model: work with local potters so the craft community benefits (co-design, fair pay, an artisan credit on the product)
- Packaging and transport protection for a heavy, fragile product

**Output:** production plan, test results, cost sheet, artisan collaboration plan.

---

## 7. Design Studio: Final Concept

**Design brief:** A contemporary martaban collection that:
1. keeps the jar's traditional strengths (opaque, glazed, thick-walled, good for oil-based pickles)
2. fixes its problems (lead-free and safe, lighter, sealed lid, sized for small kitchens)
3. keeps the heritage visible (form, motifs, artisan story)

**Possible concept features:**
- Modular sizes that stack or nest
- A gasketed lid that keeps the traditional knob form
- A matching ladle that sits in a notch in the lid, so no oily spoon goes back into the jar
- A label area or engraved band for the pickle type and date
- A story card or QR code linking to the artisan and the jar's history

**Output:** final prototypes, renders, a user test with households, a branding and exhibition display.

3. Field research: artisans, users, documentation
4. Analysis: why the traditional jar works, why it is declining, safety issues
5. Design process: drawing → shaping → production drawings

## 10. Research Questions to Answer

- How can a traditional food-storage vessel stay relevant in modern urban kitchens?
- Which features of the martaban keep pickles well, and how can design keep them?
- How can production scale up while artisans keep their role and benefit?
- How can safety (lead-free glazes) be improved without losing the heritage look?
